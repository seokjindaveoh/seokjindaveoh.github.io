About

I am a first-year Ph.D. student in Electrical and Computer Engineering at Texas A&M University. I received my B.S. and M.S. in electrical engineering from Kookmin University, where I worked with Prof. Kyeong-Sik Min on memristor neural networks for Equilibrium Propagation learning.

My work centers on on-device AI hardware: memristor crossbars, local learning algorithms such as Equilibrium Propagation, and hardware–algorithm co-design for power, performance, and area. I want neuromorphic technology to make intelligence efficient enough to run anywhere, including places with limited power.

Four levels, one system

Learning that fits the hardware

The brain learns on about 20 W, and each synapse changes using only the signals around it. I design neural networks in that spirit: small enough for an edge device, and able to learn on the chip itself.

  • Lightweight models. Quantized and ternary weights and compact networks that fit in on-chip memory, so inference does not wait on off-chip DRAM.
  • Local learning. Equilibrium Propagation trains an energy-based Hopfield network with signals available at each synapse, with no stored gradients. On memristor crossbars it used more than 80% fewer computing resources than back-propagation.
  • Modeling biological computing. Energy-based and spiking networks, and learning rules that tolerate faulty cells: with 10% defective cells, MNIST accuracy came back from 33% to 92%.

Built for power, performance and area

A learning rule is only useful once a circuit can run it. At this level the algorithm becomes analog and mixed-signal blocks, and each choice is weighed by power, performance and area (PPA).

  • Circuits shaped by the algorithm. Drivers, transimpedance amplifiers and neuron circuits that follow what the learning rule needs, such as the two phases of Equilibrium Propagation.
  • Fewer converters. A DAC/ADC-free architecture drops the CMOS neuron and converter circuits that can take more than 60% of system power.
  • Analog limits. A fully differential amplifier absorbs the offset and swing limits that otherwise shift accuracy by ±4%.
  • VLSI design. An automated flow from a trained network to schematic and layout, so PPA is checked on real designs and not only in simulation.

Parallel computing where the weights live

In a crossbar the weights never move. Input voltages on the rows multiply the cell conductances (Ohm's law) and the currents add up on each column (Kirchhoff's law), so a whole vector–matrix multiplication finishes in one step, without the memory bottleneck.

  • A neural network layer in hardware. Rows are inputs, columns are neurons, and each cross-point cell is a synapse.
  • Massive parallelism. One read performs every multiply–accumulate of the layer at once, and cells sit between two metal layers at 4F² density.
  • Real arrays are imperfect. Stuck-at faults, IR drop and sneak paths. Compensating only the most critical 5% of stuck-at cells kept VGG16 accuracy within 0.5% of the ideal network.

Synapses that remember without power

Every crossing needs a device that holds an analog weight. I have worked with RRAM, ferroelectric HZO capacitors (FeRAM), ferroelectric FETs and IGZO DRAM, choosing each for what the network needs.

  • Non-volatile. Weights stay without refresh or standby power, so a sensor node can sleep and wake up with its model intact.
  • BEOL-compatible. RRAM, HZO ferroelectrics and IGZO are made at low temperature in the back-end-of-line, stacked over CMOS logic for 3D density.
  • Low-power devices. Field-driven ferroelectric switching writes with very little current, and multi-level states store more than one bit per cell.
  • Three terminals when learning. FeFETs and IGZO transistors separate the read and write paths, which makes on-chip weight updates easier.
Vision and mission

Computing that works where power is scarce

Access to energy is becoming a question of equity. In 2019, building a healthcare IoT platform at the Qualcomm Institute, UC San Diego, I saw how much power and computing it took to send raw sensor data to the cloud, and how much of that cost came from moving data between memory and processors. Since then my work has followed one idea: process data where it is sensed, and compute where it is stored. That is what neuromorphic computing and compute-in-memory (CIM) offer, the brain's way of keeping memory and computation in the same place.

Vision
Edge neuromorphic computing that breaks the power–performance trade-off, so intelligent devices run sustainably and affordably, even where access to power is limited.
Mission
Build neuromorphic and CIM hardware that learns on the chip, by designing the device, the crossbar, the circuit and the algorithm together, and by treating power, performance and reliability as one problem instead of three.
Questions
  1. How can processing data at the edge ease the power–performance trade-off?
  2. How can emerging devices such as memristors bring the brain's efficiency to low-power on-chip computing?
  3. How can neuromorphic systems overcome the physical limits of analog circuits and stay scalable and reliable in the real world?

These questions led me from memristor crossbars at Kookmin University, through non-ideal devices, local learning and analog imperfections, to ferroelectric devices at Jeonbuk National University, and now to the Ph.D. program at Texas A&M University.
